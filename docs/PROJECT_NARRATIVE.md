## Project purpose

The project develops a specialised teaching workflow for incorporating generative artificial intelligence into urban design education. Its purpose is not to treat image generation as an isolated visual exercise. Instead, generative tools are positioned within a traceable sequence of observation, interpretation, design decision-making and evaluation.

## The central teaching question

How can students move from incomplete and multi-scale evidence about an urban environment to design alternatives that are explicit, testable and open to revision?

## One workflow across three critical phases

### Phase 1 — Site Information Collection

Students begin with the urban environment as observed. Daytime street imagery, nighttime-light information and contextual spatial data provide different views of the site. NightDiff extends the observable record by synthesising nighttime street views from daytime imagery, supporting a more comprehensive 24-hour audit.

Generated images are not accepted as evidence on appearance alone. Paired real and synthetic stimuli are compared, and a human-rating protocol assesses six dimensions: perceived safety, liveliness, beauty, wealthiness, depressing quality and boring quality. This introduces validation, uncertainty and human interpretation into the learning process.

### Phase 2 — Predictive User Behavior Analysis

The second phase asks how environmental representations relate to the use of urban space. The supplied student study is organised into three sequential analyses:

1. **Analysis 1 — Compare representations.** Satellite nighttime light and street-view-based illumination are compared and standardised. Students learn that a sensing method, viewing position and spatial resolution affect what a dataset represents.
2. **Analysis 2 — Relate to activity.** Environmental measures are examined in relation to nighttime mobile activity across spatial scales and temporal windows. The teaching emphasis is on evaluating association without claiming causation.
3. **Analysis 3 — Diagnose mismatch.** Absolute and relative disagreement is mapped and interpreted with built-environment variables, including FAR, NDVI, population density and Local Climate Zones. Recurring lighting and urban-form scenarios help turn disagreement into a spatial question for closer investigation.

The evidence types remain conceptually distinct. Human perception is not the same as measured brightness; measured brightness is not the same as activity; and activity is not the same as social interaction or safety.

### Phase 3 — Iterative Design Ideation

The third phase converts analytical questions into explicit design variables, constraints and performance criteria. The supplied MVI study demonstrates a constrained morphology search:

- Four morphology families: mixed, tower, courtyard and slab
- Fixed plot ratio: 6.0
- SCAP range: 0.10–0.30
- 100 alternatives per family and 400 alternatives in total
- Performance comparison using Mountain View Index and Site Coverage Above Podium

Students do not select a single generated image. They parameterise a design space, generate comparable alternatives, evaluate performance, retain links between parameters and outcomes, and iteratively refine selected candidates.

## How the phases connect

The project-wide chain is:

**Visual and spatial observation → human perception validation → comparison of environmental representations → relationship with observed activity → spatial mismatch diagnosis → design-variable formulation → constrained morphology generation and evaluation → renewed observation and validation**

The transition from Phase 2 to Phase 3 is a teaching integration, not a completed causal experiment. The nighttime study identifies analytical questions and possible built-environment contributors. The MVI study demonstrates a method of performance-led morphology exploration. A future integrated teaching exercise can select design alternatives, represent them under day and night conditions, invite perception ratings, compare those responses with design-performance criteria and return the results to the next design iteration.

## Evidence status used on the website

The public page uses three labels:

- **Existing project output:** material supplied as a completed project tool or demonstration.
- **Selected student study:** completed teaching work supplied for inclusion as a course outcome.
- **Integrated teaching demonstration:** a new connection proposed to show how existing outputs form one reusable workflow.

These labels prevent the narrative from extending beyond the available evidence.

## Intended learning outcomes

After completing the workflow, students should be able to:

- distinguish environmental representations by scale, viewpoint and meaning;
- use generative imagery as a testable component of site observation;
- incorporate human perception without equating it with physical measurement;
- interpret associations and spatial mismatch without overclaiming causality;
- translate an urban diagnosis into controllable design variables and constraints;
- generate, evaluate and iteratively refine urban morphology alternatives; and
- document evidence boundaries and uncertainty in computational urban design.
