## The City as a Learning Loop

The project develops a specialised teaching workflow for incorporating generative artificial intelligence into urban design education. GenAI is not treated as an isolated image-making exercise. It is positioned within a traceable loop of observation, reconstruction, validation, interpretation, design generation and renewed evaluation.

The central teaching question is: **How can students move from incomplete and multi-scale evidence about an urban environment to design alternatives that are explicit, testable and open to revision?**

The core teaching proposition is:

> Urban design becomes iterative when students can reconstruct what conventional data miss, verify those representations, connect them to lived behaviour, generate alternatives and test what those alternatives change.

## 1. A Dynamic City, a Static Evidence Base

Cities change across hours, viewpoints, social groups and environmental conditions. Site information is nevertheless often dominated by a static daytime record. The first learning action is therefore to **reveal the gap**: students identify which urban conditions are visible, which are absent and why those absences matter for design.

## 2. What the City Does Not Show Us

Different sensing systems expose different urban realities. Satellite imagery, street views, measured illuminance, human perception and mobile activity are not interchangeable. Students learn to ask what each representation can support before using it as evidence.

This framing establishes **Phase 1 — Site Information Collection** as more than data acquisition. It is a critical audit of the evidence base.

## 3. Reconstructing the Missing City

NightDiff is used as a research prototype for reconstructing nighttime street conditions from paired day–night evidence and multimodal inputs. Representative comparisons make the model process visible. Synthetic scenes extend what students can inspect when repeated or comprehensive nighttime observation is constrained.

Generation does not turn absence into fact. The generated view is a hypothesis about a missing condition, not a substitute for field evidence.

## 4. Synthetic Evidence Must Earn Trust

The workflow **grounds** generated evidence through multiple forms of comparison. Model outputs are compared with reference scenes and image-derived estimates are checked against measured illuminance. A human-rating protocol adds six perceptual dimensions: perceived safety, liveliness, beauty, wealthiness, depressing quality and boring quality.

Physical validation and human judgement answer different questions. Neither is used as a universal measure of urban quality.

## 5. From Appearance to Lived Experience

**Phase 2 — Predictive User Behavior Analysis** moves from how the city appears to how urban space is used. A selected student nighttime-audit study first establishes a diagnostic sequence:

1. **Analysis 1 — Compare representations.** Satellite nighttime light and street-view-based illumination are compared and standardised.
2. **Analysis 2 — Relate to activity.** Environmental measures are examined in relation to nighttime mobile activity across spatial and temporal scales.
3. **Analysis 3 — Diagnose mismatch.** Absolute and relative disagreement is mapped and interpreted with FAR, NDVI, population density, Local Climate Zones and lighting configuration.

A separate satellite-to-bike-demand research prototype demonstrates the proposal-defined capability of predicting how people may use space. It makes visible an explicit chain from satellite imagery and public spatial data to model comparison, travel-intensity or bikeshare-demand estimates and mapped outputs.

The student study and predictive prototype are complementary examples within the same proposal-defined phase. They are not presented as the same dataset or experiment.

## 6. Evidence Becomes a Design Brief

Mismatch becomes valuable when it changes the next design question. Students translate an urban diagnosis into controllable variables, planning constraints and explicit criteria. This is the hinge between analysis and intervention: a map becomes a reasoned design brief rather than a decorative conclusion.

## 7. Generating and Comparing Alternatives

**Phase 3 — Iterative Design Ideation** begins with a selected student MVI study that translates the iterative principle into a traceable parametric search:

- Four morphology families: mixed, tower, courtyard and slab
- Fixed plot ratio: 6.0
- SCAP range: 0.10–0.30
- 100 alternatives per family and 400 alternatives in total
- Performance comparison using Mountain View Index and Site Coverage Above Podium

After students learn to generate and evaluate traceable alternatives, a separate project prototype extends the same logic to generative scenario testing, three-dimensional reconstruction and environmental-performance feedback. It demonstrates how comparable morphology scenarios can return evidence to the next design decision rather than resolve into one final image.

The MVI study is a student outcome in performance-led parametric design. It is not described as a model-generated dataset or as a direct empirical solution to the nighttime case.

## 8. The Proposal Returns to the Loop

Evaluation is not the end of the workflow. Selected alternatives can be represented under day and night conditions, assessed through human perception and physical-performance criteria, and returned to another cycle of observation and design refinement.

This **re-audit** is an integrated teaching demonstration. It defines how the supplied outputs can form one reusable loop without claiming that every source was produced in one continuous experiment.

## 9. A Transferable Teaching Practice

After completing the workflow, students should be able to:

- distinguish environmental representations by scale, viewpoint and meaning;
- use generative imagery as a testable component of site observation;
- incorporate physical validation and human perception without conflating them;
- interpret prediction, association and spatial mismatch without overclaiming causality;
- translate diagnosis into controllable design variables and constraints;
- generate, compare and iteratively refine urban morphology alternatives; and
- document evidence boundaries and uncertainty in computational urban design.

## 10. Open Resources and Responsible Reuse

The public page uses three evidence labels:

- **Research Prototype:** a supplied model, workflow or model-based result.
- **Validation Evidence:** a supplied comparison that tests a representation or design output.
- **Student Outcome:** completed teaching work selected to demonstrate how the method can be learned and applied.

Full source presentations, editable figures, raw trajectories and restricted data remain outside the public repository. Public assets are reduced-resolution previews and remain subject to the project sharing checklist.
